Olive Ridley Turtles

Why in News?
Olive Ridley turtles were recently in news due to large-scale nesting and conservation efforts along India’s eastern coastline.

About Olive Ridley Turtles:

  • Scientific name: Lepidochelys olivacea.
  • They are the most abundant sea turtle species in the world.
  • Named after their olive-green coloured carapace.

Habitat & Distribution:

  • Found in tropical and subtropical waters of the Pacific, Indian, and Atlantic Oceans.
  • In India, major nesting sites include:
    • Gahirmatha Beach (Odisha)
    • Rushikulya coast
    • Devi River mouth

Unique Feature:

  • Known for mass nesting called “Arribada”, where thousands of turtles come ashore simultaneously.

Conservation Status:

  • IUCN Red List: Vulnerable.
  • Wildlife (Protection) Act, 1972: Schedule I.
  • Protected under international conventions like CITES and CMS.

Threats:

  • Fishing nets and trawling leading to accidental deaths.
  • Coastal development and habitat loss.
  • Marine pollution and climate change.

Conservation Efforts:

  • Operation Olivia by Indian Coast Guard to protect nesting sites.
  • Use of Turtle Excluder Devices (TEDs) in fishing nets.
  • Community participation and beach protection measures.

Wheat Crop – Climatic Conditions

Why in News?
Rising temperatures and changing climatic conditions are affecting wheat production in India, raising concerns over food security.

Climatic Conditions Required for Wheat:

  • Wheat is a temperate crop grown in cool seasons.
  • Requires cool and moist climate during growth and warm, dry climate at maturity.

Temperature:

  • Optimum temperature for growth: 21°C to 24°C.
  • Can tolerate range: 4°C to 35°C.
  • High temperature during flowering leads to reduced yield.

Rainfall:

  • Requires about 30–38 cm rainfall during growing season.
  • Excess rainfall can lead to diseases and crop damage.

Sunlight:

  • Needs bright sunshine during grain ripening.

Soil Requirements:

  • Best suited to well-drained loamy soil.
  • Requires good availability of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

Impact of Climate Change:

  • Rising temperature causes early maturity and reduced grain filling.
  • Heat stress during flowering significantly lowers productivity.
  • Erratic rainfall affects sowing and yield stability.

Pushkarmema curajae

Why in News?
Researchers from the Central University of Rajasthan have recently discovered a new species of cyanobacteria named Pushkarmema curajae from Pushkar Lake in Ajmer.

About Pushkarmema curajae:

  • It is a newly identified species of cyanobacteria.
  • Discovered in Pushkar Lake (Ajmer, Rajasthan).
  • The genus name "Pushkarmema" is derived from Pushkar Lake.
  • The species name "curajae" honours the Central University of Rajasthan.
  • It has potential applications in:
    • Drug development
    • Biofertilizers
    • Nutrient-rich supplements to boost immunity

Key Facts about Cyanobacteria:

  • They are photosynthetic microscopic organisms found in aquatic environments.
  • Commonly called blue-green algae (though not true algae).
  • They are prokaryotic organisms (lack a true nucleus).
  • Found in freshwater, marine, and brackish water.
  • Capable of photosynthesis and oxygen production.
  • Many species can fix atmospheric nitrogen, enriching soil fertility.
  • Among the oldest life forms on Earth (~3.5 billion years old).

Significance:

  • Important for ecological balance and nutrient cycling.
  • Useful in agriculture (biofertilizers).
  • Potential role in biotechnology and pharmaceuticals.

Leaf Spot Diseases

Why in News?
Leaf spot disease in arecanut plantations has emerged as a major concern, and government institutes in Karnataka are conducting field demonstrations to guide farmers on effective management practices.

About Leaf Spot Diseases:

  • Leaf spot diseases refer to a group of plant diseases caused by fungi, bacteria, or other microorganisms.
  • These pathogens infect the leaf surface and produce visible spots or lesions.
  • The spots can vary in size, shape, and color and may coalesce in severe cases.
  • They interfere with photosynthesis, affecting plant growth and productivity.

Causes:

  • Majorly caused by fungi (about 85%) and also by bacteria.
  • Environmental factors like:
    • Water stress
    • Sunscald
    • Chemical injury (pesticides/herbicides)
    • Nutrient imbalance
    can produce similar symptoms.

Symptoms:

  • Appearance of small to large spots or lesions on leaves.
  • Leaves may turn yellow or brown.
  • Severe infection leads to shriveling and premature leaf drop.

Distribution & Occurrence:

  • Occurs in almost all regions of the world.
  • More prevalent in humid and wet climates.
  • Affects:
    • Vegetables
    • Fruit trees
    • Ornamental plants
    • Shrubs

Impact:

  • Leads to reduced crop productivity.
  • Poses a threat to food security and economic stability.
  • Can severely damage plant foliage if untreated.

Control & Prevention:

  • Use of cultural practices (crop rotation, sanitation).
  • Biological control methods.
  • Judicious use of chemical fungicides.
  • Integrated disease management is most effective.

Why in News?

About Cerium–Magnesium Changesite:

  • It is a newly discovered mineral from the Moon.
  • It is the 11th known lunar mineral.
  • Discovered in a lunar meteorite named Pakepake 005, found in China.
  • The meteorite is a small spherical rock (~44 grams) with a molten outer layer.

Physical Characteristics:

  • Colourless, transparent, and brittle mineral.
  • Extremely small in size: 3–25 micrometres (mostly <10 µm).

Key Features:

  • Exhibits fluorescence (emits light under certain conditions).
  • Contains rare earth elements along with magnesium and iron.
  • Its composition helps understand Moon’s surface and interior processes.
  • Variations in crystal structure provide insights into mineral formation under extreme conditions.

Significance:

  • Helps in understanding lunar geology and evolution.
  • Fluorescent property may have applications in advanced technologies like next-generation LEDs.
  • Important for studying extraterrestrial mineral diversity.

Sea Slugs

Why in News?
A rare sea slug species Discodorid cebuensis has been recorded in India for the first time after being spotted near Haji Ali, Mumbai.

About Sea Slugs:

  • Sea slugs are soft-bodied marine molluscs found across all oceans, from shallow waters to the deep sea.
  • They belong to Phylum Mollusca and Class Gastropoda.
  • They resemble snails but lack external shells (naked snails).
  • Also called Nudibranchs or ocean slugs.

Habitat:

  • Commonly found in coral reef ecosystems.
  • Their presence indicates a healthy marine ecosystem.

Feeding Behaviour:

  • Feed on algae, sponges, and small invertebrates.
  • Some species store stinging cells of jellyfish for defense.

Key Features:

  • Known for bright colours and patterns (warning signals to predators).
  • Many are toxic, deriving toxins from prey.
  • Have sensory tentacles to detect chemicals and movement.
  • Some species can photosynthesize using sunlight.
  • Capable of regeneration of body parts.
  • Few species can even incorporate genes from prey.

Behaviour:

  • Most are diurnal, though some species are nocturnal.

Forest Soils Identified as Hidden Methane Sinks: New Study

Context: Scientists have discovered that forest soils actively contribute to climate mitigation by absorbing methane (CH₄), a highly potent greenhouse gas.

Key Discovery:

  • Forest soils are not passive but actively remove greenhouse gases.
  • They absorb methane (CH₄), which is significantly more potent than CO₂.
  • This methane absorption capacity is increasing under certain climate conditions.

Scientific Findings:

  • Based on a long-term study (up to 24 years) across forest sites in Germany.
  • Under warmer and drier conditions, soils show increased methane uptake.
  • Methane absorption increased by approximately 3% per year.

Why Methane Matters:

  • Methane (CH₄) is about 25–28 times more potent than CO₂ in the short term.
  • Even small reductions in methane significantly impact climate change mitigation.

Role of Forest Soils:

  • Forest soils store up to ~70% of total forest carbon.
  • Now also confirmed as:
    • Carbon sink
    • Methane sink

Mechanism:

  • Special microbes called methanotrophs consume methane.
  • Oxygen-rich soils enhance methane absorption.
  • Warm and dry conditions boost microbial activity.

Limitations:

  • Benefit occurs only under specific environmental conditions.
  • Excess moisture reduces methane uptake.
  • Extreme climate change may still damage forest ecosystems.

Threats:

  • Deforestation and logging
  • Soil disturbance due to land-use change
  • Altered rainfall patterns

Significance:

  • Suggests climate models may underestimate forests’ role.
  • Highlights importance of soil conservation.
  • Helps improve climate predictions and forest management strategies.

Policy Implications:

  • Protect undisturbed forest ecosystems
  • Minimize soil disturbance
  • Promote natural forests over monocultures
  • Increase investment in soil research

Conclusion: Forest soils act as a hidden climate regulator by functioning as both carbon and methane sinks, strengthening Earth’s natural climate defense system.
