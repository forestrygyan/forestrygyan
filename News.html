Shekha Jheel Bird Sanctuary Declared India’s 99th Ramsar Site

Context: Recently, the Shekha Jheel Bird Sanctuary in Uttar Pradesh has been designated as a Ramsar Site, increasing India’s total Ramsar sites to 99.

Location:

  • Located in Aligarh district, Uttar Pradesh.
  • Part of the Gangetic plains wetland ecosystem.

About the Sanctuary:

  • A freshwater perennial wetland formed after the construction of the Upper Ganges Canal (1852).
  • Represents a typical monsoonal wetland ecosystem.
  • Acts as an important stopover along the Central Asian Flyway.

Flora (Vegetation):

  • Submerged: Hydrilla, Vallisneria, Ceratophyllum
  • Floating: Azolla, Salvinia, Eichhornia
  • Rooted floating: Nymphoides species

Fauna:

  • Important habitat for migratory birds such as:
    • Bar-headed Goose
    • Painted Stork
    • Various species of ducks

What is a Ramsar Site?

  • Wetland designated under the Ramsar Convention (1971).
  • Recognized for international importance in biodiversity conservation.
  • Supports ecosystem services like flood control, groundwater recharge, and climate regulation.

Significance:

  • Strengthens India’s commitment to wetland conservation.
  • Enhances protection of migratory bird habitats.
  • Highlights importance of wetlands in climate resilience.

UPSC Relevance:

  • Important for Ramsar Sites, Wetlands, Biodiversity
  • Frequently asked in Prelims (location-based questions)

Conclusion: The inclusion of Shekha Jheel as a Ramsar Site underlines the ecological importance of wetlands in sustaining biodiversity and supporting climate stability.

Stauranthera aureoglossa: New ‘Golden-Tongued’ Plant Discovered in Arunachal Pradesh

Context: A team of botanists has discovered a new plant species named Stauranthera aureoglossa in the Eastern Himalayas of Arunachal Pradesh.

Location:

  • Found in Kamle and Upper Subansiri districts of Arunachal Pradesh.
  • Occurs in mid-elevation evergreen forests along shaded streams.

About the Species:

  • A perennial herb growing on damp rocks and soil near streams.
  • Belongs to a group adapted to moist, shaded habitats.

Key Features:

  • Distinct bell-shaped bluish-purple flower.
  • Lacks the typical tubular projection (spur) seen in related species.
  • Has a unique yellow, tongue-like structure inside petals → gives the name “aureoglossa”.
  • Leaves are diamond or spear-shaped.

Conservation Status:

  • Provisionally classified as endangered.
  • Habitat is fragile and restricted.

Threats:

  • Logging activities
  • Landslides
  • Road widening projects

Significance:

  • Highlights rich biodiversity of the Eastern Himalaya hotspot.
  • Emphasizes need for conservation of microhabitats.

UPSC Relevance:

  • Important for species discovery, endemism, biodiversity conservation.
  • Can be used in Prelims (species-based questions) and Mains answers.

Conclusion: The discovery of Stauranthera aureoglossa underlines the ecological importance of fragile Himalayan ecosystems and the urgent need for their protection.

Forest Soils Identified as Hidden Methane Sinks: New Study

Context: Scientists have discovered that forest soils actively contribute to climate mitigation by absorbing methane (CH₄), a highly potent greenhouse gas.

Key Discovery:

  • Forest soils are not passive but actively remove greenhouse gases.
  • They absorb methane (CH₄), which is significantly more potent than CO₂.
  • This methane absorption capacity is increasing under certain climate conditions.

Scientific Findings:

  • Based on a long-term study (up to 24 years) across forest sites in Germany.
  • Under warmer and drier conditions, soils show increased methane uptake.
  • Methane absorption increased by approximately 3% per year.

Why Methane Matters:

  • Methane (CH₄) is about 25–28 times more potent than CO₂ in the short term.
  • Even small reductions in methane significantly impact climate change mitigation.

Role of Forest Soils:

  • Forest soils store up to ~70% of total forest carbon.
  • Now also confirmed as:
    • Carbon sink
    • Methane sink

Mechanism:

  • Special microbes called methanotrophs consume methane.
  • Oxygen-rich soils enhance methane absorption.
  • Warm and dry conditions boost microbial activity.

Limitations:

  • Benefit occurs only under specific environmental conditions.
  • Excess moisture reduces methane uptake.
  • Extreme climate change may still damage forest ecosystems.

Threats:

  • Deforestation and logging
  • Soil disturbance due to land-use change
  • Altered rainfall patterns

Significance:

  • Suggests climate models may underestimate forests’ role.
  • Highlights importance of soil conservation.
  • Helps improve climate predictions and forest management strategies.

Policy Implications:

  • Protect undisturbed forest ecosystems
  • Minimize soil disturbance
  • Promote natural forests over monocultures
  • Increase investment in soil research

Conclusion: Forest soils act as a hidden climate regulator by functioning as both carbon and methane sinks, strengthening Earth’s natural climate defense system.

India’s forests could nearly double carbon storage by 2100, study finds

Context: A recent study has found that India’s forests could significantly increase their carbon storage capacity by the end of the century due to changing climate conditions and emission scenarios.

Key Findings:

  • Forest carbon storage may increase by up to 97% by 2100 under high-emission scenarios.
  • National average forest carbon could rise substantially compared to historical levels.
  • Increase is projected across different emission pathways (low, medium, high).

Regional Variation:

  • Semi-arid and dry regions may see higher increases in carbon storage.
  • Ecologically rich regions like the Western Ghats and Eastern Himalayas may show only modest gains.

Concerns:

  • Higher carbon storage does not necessarily mean healthier forests.
  • Climate change may increase risks such as droughts, wildfires, and ecosystem stress.
  • Dense forests may become more vulnerable despite increased biomass.

Key Drivers:

  • Rainfall is a major factor influencing forest carbon growth.
  • With higher emissions, temperature becomes a dominant factor.

UPSC Relevance:

  • Important for topics: Carbon Sink, Climate Change, Forest Ecology
  • Useful example for answers on nature-based solutions and climate mitigation
